# LLMs replace the mental worker's illusion of authorship with the illusion that capital itself is productive

Mental workers under capitalism have long operated under a particular mystification: that they author and own their products. Abstracted from social relations of production, the individual who writes, codes, or designs appears to produce directly. This is not pure falsehood — as an isolated concrete individual, the mental worker does produce. The illusion lies in treating this concrete individual reality as the whole of the social picture.

Large language models introduce a historically significant shift: they replace this authorship illusion with a different mystification, one with deeper precedent in Marx's analysis of capital as fetish. When an LLM generates text, code, or analysis, the appearance is that capital — the accumulated technological investment, the infrastructure — is itself productive. The social labor of millions of writers, coders, and thinkers whose outputs trained the model disappears behind the model's outputs.

What makes this analysis precise is that the second illusion is also real at its own level of abstraction. Capital as social relation — as the accumulation of dead labor, crystallized knowledge, and organizational form — does mediate and enable production. The fetish has a material basis. The error is not that capital appears productive; the error is that the social relations constituting that productivity vanish from view.

The shift from the first illusion to the second represents a historical deepening: from "I produce, therefore I own" to "capital produces, therefore capital earns." Each step removes the individual worker further from visibility in the production process. The mental worker who once claimed authorship now stands behind a machine that claims productivity for capital.

This is not merely a change in ideology but a change in the material conditions producing that ideology. LLMs are not simply a rhetorical device for capital; they are a real mechanism by which social labor becomes invisible as it is absorbed into capital's productive apparatus.
